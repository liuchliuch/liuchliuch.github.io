01 / THE QUESTION

Finding one. Counting them all.

Imagine a timetable with no clashes. Finding one answers the question “Is this possible?” Counting asks something more: how many different timetables satisfy the rules?

The same distinction appears in coloring maps, matching workers to tasks, and describing configurations of a physical system. Checking a proposed answer can be easy. Counting every answer may demand a very different idea.

A name for this: #P#P is a complexity class of counting functions whose solutions can be checked efficiently.

Our six-point example is small enough to list every configuration. With 100 points, there are already 2100 on/off patterns. Counting complexity asks: when does the structure let us avoid examining them one by one?

↳

A huge number of possibilities does not by itself make counting hard. With no connections, the answer for n points is simply 2n. The structure of the rules is what matters.

02 / A COMMON LANGUAGE

When possibilities carry weight.

Ordinary counting gives every valid answer a contribution of 1. In many models, different answers carry different weights. Adding those contributions gives a partition function.

From counting to evaluationThe example below uses nonnegative real weights. In research, weights can also be complex numbers, allowing contributions to cancel.

Return to the six-point cycle. Give each point that is on a weight λ. A configuration with k points on then contributes λk. Move the slider to turn a count into a weighted sum.

Local rules supply contributions; the whole problem sums over assignments. This shared pattern connects questions about constraints, graphs, and networks—and gives their proofs common ground.

03 / THE RESEARCH LANDSCAPE

Which structures make counting tractable?

An algorithm solves a class of problems. A classification theorem asks a broader question: across a whole family, which cases admit efficient algorithms, and which are #P-hard? CountingLib connects these results and their reusable mathematics across the following directions.

What “hard” means#P-hardness is stated through reductions. It does not unconditionally rule out efficient algorithms; the papers give the exact scope.

BOOLEAN HOLANT

Local functions. A global sum.

Place a function at each junction of a network, then sum over assignments to its wires. Boolean Holant dichotomies classify the function families that permit efficient evaluation and those that lead to #P-hardness.

COMPLEX-WEIGHTED CSP

Recognizing what the rules allow.

Variables interact through weighted local constraints. The research addresses both the classification of evaluation problems and effective recognition of which case a given collection of rules belongs to.

PLANAR GRAPH HOMOMORPHISMS

How many ways can one graph fit another?

Assign a type to each vertex while respecting which types may be neighbors. Graph coloring is a familiar example. The library includes planar homomorphism dichotomies for nonnegative algebraic weights.

MATCHGATE WIDTH

How compact can an exact representation be?

A complicated counting object may admit a smaller exact representation. Matchgate-width results explore the power and limits of this compression, including qutrit trichotomy and exact compression.

04 / FROM RESEARCH TO A LIBRARY

Proofs that other proofs can build on.

A paper explains why a result is true. Lean is a proof assistant: it checks the logical steps of a formal proof against precise definitions and assumptions. Those proofs can then become parts of new results.

New to Lean?Think of it as a language for mathematics with a rigorous logical checker.Meet Lean ↗

CountingLib connects research into a reusable system. Shared definitions describe the objects being counted. Algorithms specify what they compute. Bridge theorems connect models. Classification results retain their precise scope.

Computing the right value and proving a running-time bound are different guarantees. CountingLib states them separately, so the next piece of research can see exactly what it uses and what it assumes.
