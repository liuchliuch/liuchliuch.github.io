Near-Optimal Label Complexity
for Active Huber Regression

Abstract

We resolve, up to logarithmic factors, the Huber dimension-dependence question posed by Musco, Musco, Woodruff, and Yasuda [14], reducing the rank exponent from 4−2⁢2 to one. We study fixed-design active regression in the exact-real label-query model: the feature matrix A∈Rn×d is known, while an arbitrary response vector is accessible only through coordinate queries. For r=rank⁡(A), our randomized nonadaptive algorithm queries min⁡{n,O~⁢(r/ε2)} labels and, with probability at least 0.99, returns a (1+ε)-approximation to the square root of the empirical Huber loss. All query locations are fixed before any label is read. For every admissible pool size and rank, and 0<ε≤1/800, we construct a fixed rank-r design on which every adaptive randomized algorithm with the same worst-case guarantee needs Ω⁡(min⁡{n,r/ε2}) labels in worst-case expectation. Thus label complexity is jointly optimal in pool size, rank, and accuracy up to logarithmic factors in this range. The guarantee requires no distributional or noise assumptions and includes zero-optimum instances. The key ingredient is a centered affine transfer lemma: design-only multiscale scores preserve the objective increments needed to locate the minimizer after an independent constant-factor pilot. This overcomes the hidden-label dependence of standard affine sparsification and turns near-linear design sparsification into a near-optimal active algorithm.

1 Introduction

The Huber loss is quadratic for small residuals and linear for large ones, combining the local efficiency of least squares with bounded influence in the tails [9]. It is consequently a standard robust surrogate in statistics and machine learning. In large pools of unlabeled examples, however, observing a response may itself be the dominant cost: a label can require a human annotation, a physical experiment, or an expensive simulation. This motivates active regression: the feature matrix is available in full, but the algorithm should fit the responses after inspecting as few of them as possible.

We study the strongest transductive version of this question. The design A∈Rn×d is arbitrary and known, the response b∈Rn is arbitrary and hidden, and one query reveals one coordinate of b. There is no distributional model, no stochastic-noise assumption, and no promise that the optimum is bounded away from zero. The target is a relative approximation to the empirical Huber objective on the entire fixed pool. This worst-case formulation sharply separates label complexity from the statistical literature on Huber regression, where samples are drawn from a population and robustness is measured through estimation or prediction risk [18].

For squared loss, geometric sampling gives label complexity linear in the effective dimension for constant accuracy [4]. Lewis-weight methods likewise give nearly tight guarantees for least absolute deviations [2, 15]. Huber regression is harder because it is not homogeneous: the same direction can behave quadratically at one radius and linearly at another. Musco, Musco, Woodruff, and Yasuda developed a general active-regression framework. After the standard reduction to the column space, their Huber specialization uses O~⁢(r4−2⁢2⁢poly⁡(1/ε)) queries, where r=rank⁡(A) [14]. Their dimension-reduction discussion motivates asking whether the exponent of this effective dimension can be reduced to one.

At first sight, the near-linear unshifted Huber sparsifier of Jambulapati et al. [12] appears to settle the question. It does not. A standard affine reduction replaces each row by (ai,−bi), so the importance scores of the resulting sparsifier inspect precisely the labels that an active algorithm is trying not to query. Nor can one hope for a label-independent strong affine coreset for every response vector: an unqueried coordinate can hide an arbitrarily large constant term. The algorithm must preserve less than the whole objective, but enough to locate its minimizer.

Our results.

We give a nonadaptive, label-oblivious algorithm with query complexity

min⁡{n,O~⁢(r/ε2)},r=rank⁡(A), (1.1)

and constant success probability. After the same column-space reduction, the advance is the linear, rather than r4−2⁢2, rank dependence together with the joint dependence on n and ε. The sampling locations are fixed before any response is read. Our lower bound holds for the same approximation criterion even against adaptive randomized algorithms: for every admissible triple (n,d,r) and 0<ε≤1/800, some fixed rank-r design requires

Ω⁡(min⁡{n,rε2}) (1.2)

queries in worst-case expectation. Hence, in this small-error range, (1.1) is optimal simultaneously in sample size, rank, and accuracy up to logarithmic factors. In particular, the full-query cap is not merely an artifact of the algorithm: when the pool is too small to support r/ε2 informative responses, a fixed positive fraction of the entire pool can be necessary.

The technical contribution behind the upper bound is a centered affine transfer principle. From A alone we construct one multiscale score vector of total mass O⁡(r⁢log⁡n). For any fixed residual z, sampling by these scores uniformly preserves the increments of the translated objective Fz⁢(h):=∑iH⁡(zi+aiT⁢h) over the relevant unshifted Huber ball. The baseline Fz⁢(0), which may contain unseen and arbitrarily large labels, cancels. An independent pilot supplies a constant-factor center, and the centered estimate then localizes its own unconstrained minimizer. The complete query set is chosen before any label is read.

The pilot, residualization, and localization steps originate in Musco et al. [14]; the multiscale unshifted sparsifier comes from Jambulapati et al. [12]; and the common-basepoint chaining principle is already present in Jambulapati et al. [11]. Our use of these ingredients is specific: the design-only multiscale scores control centered affine increments after an independent pilot, avoiding label-dependent augmented rows. We do not claim that centering, localization, or Huber sparsification is new in isolation.

The lower bound has two regimes. When n/r is large, independent biased-Bernoulli blocks of size N and bias β=max⁡{200⁢ε,10/N} encode r hidden signs. A relative Huber solution decodes almost all signs, while each adaptively selected response carries only O⁡(β2)=O⁡(ε2+1/N) information. When n/r is small, a realizable instance has zero optimum, so a multiplicative guarantee forces exact recovery of r=Ω⁡(n) independent labels. This second regime is what turns the earlier fixed-row-count construction into the all-n minimax statement.

2 Problem, model, and main results

This section fixes the loss, query model, and approximation convention before stating the two minimax bounds. It also records the width scaling used to reduce the analysis to the width-one loss.

For the width-one Huber loss, write

H⁡(t):={t2/2,|t|≤1,|t|−1/2,|t|>1,Fb⁢(x):=∑i=1nH⁡(aiT⁢x−bi), (2.1)

where aiT is row i of A. Following the active regression literature, we call Fb⁢(x)1/2 the Huber norm objective, although this terminology is only shorthand for the square root of the loss. The algorithm knows A in advance and learns bi only by querying coordinate i. It may perform unrestricted computation on A and on queried labels; in particular, the results below concern label complexity, not total running time, and permit exact minimization of finite convex Huber objectives.

A query count always means the number of distinct response coordinates inspected: repeated requests are answered from a cache, and a run that never returns has query count Q=∞. Absolute constants below are universal unless a dependence is displayed, and [k]:={1,…,k} for a positive integer k. A (1+ε)-approximation to Fb1/2 is equivalently a (1+ε)2-approximation to Fb.

After rank reduction, Theorem 1.5 of Musco et al. [14] uses O~⁢(r4−2⁢2⁢poly⁡(1/ε)) queries. We ask for a design-only, preferably nonadaptive query set with linear rank exponent and the joint (n,r,ε) guarantee

Fb⁢(x^)1/2≤(1+ε)⁢minx∈Rd⁢Fb⁢(x)1/2. (2.2)

The first result supplies such a nonadaptive set; its explicit logarithms concern label complexity and do not imply a running-time bound.

Theorem 2.1 (Nonadaptive upper bound).

Let A∈Rn×d, let r=rank⁡(A), and let 0<ε<1/2. There is a randomized algorithm such that the following holds for every fixed b∈Rn. Its queried coordinates are fixed before any label is read and depend only on A, ε, and private randomness. It queries at most

min⁡{n,C⁢r⁢(log6⁡(2⁢n)+log4⁡(2⁢n)ε2)} (2.3)

distinct coordinates of b and, with probability at least 0.99 over its private randomness, returns x^ satisfying

Fb⁢(x^)1/2≤(1+ε)⁢minx∈Rd⁢Fb⁢(x)1/2.

In the small-error range below, the upper bound is optimal up to logarithmic factors throughout the (n,d,r) parameter range, even if adaptivity is allowed.

Theorem 2.2 (Matching adaptive lower bound).

There is an absolute constant c>0 such that the following holds. For all integers n,d,r with 1≤r≤min⁡{n,d} and every 0<ε≤1/800, there is a fixed matrix A∈Rn×d of rank exactly r such that every adaptive randomized algorithm that, for every fixed b∈Rn, returns x^ satisfying Fb⁢(x^)1/2≤(1+ε)⁢minx⁢Fb⁢(x)1/2 with probability at least 0.99 obeys

supb∈RnER⁢Q⁢(A,b)≥c⁢min⁡{n,rε2}. (2.4)

Here Q may be a stopping-time query count and R is the algorithm’s private seed. Specifically, Q counts distinct queried coordinates. Repeated requests are permitted but can be answered from the algorithm’s cache and are not charged twice; set Q=∞ on a run that never returns an output.

Combining the two bounds gives the minimax statement in the common accuracy range. The response remains worst case, while the expectation below is over the algorithm’s private randomness.

Corollary 2.3 (Minimax label complexity).

Fix 0<ε≤1/800 and 1≤r≤min⁡{n,d}. Let A range over randomized algorithms that, for every fixed pair (A,b), achieve the (1+ε)-approximation to Fb1/2 in (2.2) with probability at least 0.99, and define

Qn,d,r(ε):=infAsupA∈Rn×d:rank⁡(A)=rb∈RnERQA(A,b), (2.5)

where the expectation is over all algorithmic randomness. For absolute constants c,C>0,

c⁢min⁡{n,rε2} ≤Qn,d,r⁢(ε)
≤min⁡{n,C⁢r⁢(log6⁡(2⁢n)+log4⁡(2⁢n)ε2)}. (2.6)

The upper algorithm is nonadaptive, whereas the lower bound holds against adaptive algorithms. Thus the minimax label complexity is Θ~⁢(min⁡{n,r/ε2}).

Proof.

Apply Theorem 2.2; for the reverse inequality use Theorem 2.1 and its full-query branch, then take the indicated infimum and supremum. ∎

For a width parameter s>0, we use the fixed-tail-slope convention

Hs⁢(t):=s⁢H⁢(t/s)={t2/(2⁢s),|t|≤s,|t|−s/2,|t|>s. (2.7)

If Fs,A,b⁢(x):=∑iHs⁢(aiT⁢x−bi), then

Fs,A,b⁢(x)=s⁢F1,A/s,b/s⁢(x).

Thus applying Theorem 2.1 to (A/s,b/s), or multiplying both the rows and labels in the hard instance of Theorem 2.2 by s, proves the same approximation and query bounds for Hs.

Proof mechanism.

One O⁡(r⁢log⁡n)-mass multiscale score vector controls unshifted Huber balls; after an independent pilot, the same scores preserve centered affine increments and localize the sampled minimizer. The lower bound uses noiseless recovery or biased-Bernoulli blocks with bounded information per queried label.

3 The nonadaptive upper bound

The upper bound has three components. We construct one design-only score vector for every unshifted radius, transfer it to centered affine increments, and combine that transfer with an independent pilot in a two-batch algorithm. The full proof is given here; the deterministic weight construction, endpoint algebra, and attainment details appear in Appendix A.

3.1 Unshifted geometry and label-oblivious scores

We begin with the metric-like square root ψH⁢(t):=H⁡(t). Its triangle and increment inequalities drive both the score construction and the later localization argument.

Lemma 3.1 (Huber triangle and increment inequalities).

For every s,t∈R,

ψH⁢(s+t) ≤ψH⁢(s)+ψH⁢(t), (3.1)
|ψH⁢(s)−ψH⁢(t)| ≤ψH⁢(s−t), (3.2)
|H⁡(s)−H⁡(t)| ≤H⁡(s−t)⁢(H⁡(s)+H⁡(t)). (3.3)

Consequently, for nonnegative weights wi and vectors y,z of the same finite dimension,

(∑iwi⁢H⁢(yi+zi))1/2≤(∑iwi⁢H⁢(yi))1/2+(∑iwi⁢H⁢(zi))1/2. (3.4)
Proof.

On [0,∞),

ψH⁢(u)={u/2,0≤u≤1,u−1/2,u≥1

is nonnegative, increasing, concave, and zero at the origin. Its increments are therefore decreasing, so ψH⁢(u+v)−ψH⁢(u)≤ψH⁢(v)−ψH⁢(0) for u,v≥0. Together with |s+t|≤|s|+|t|, this proves (3.1). Apply that inequality to s=t+(s−t), and then exchange s,t, to obtain (3.2). Factoring

|H⁡(s)−H⁡(t)|=|ψH⁢(s)−ψH⁢(t)|⁢(ψH⁢(s)+ψH⁢(t))

gives (3.3). Finally, apply (3.1) coordinatewise and use the weighted Euclidean triangle inequality to obtain (3.4). ∎

For the upper-bound lemmas, take a matrix A∈RN×r with full column rank and nonzero rows aiT. Define the unshifted objective and its sublevel sets by

Φ⁡(h):=∑i=1NH⁡(aiT⁢h),KR:={h∈Rr:Φ⁡(h)≤R}. (3.5)

Because cA:=min‖h‖2=1⁡‖A⁢h‖1>0 and H⁡(t)≥|t|−1/2,

Φ⁡(h)≥cA⁢‖h‖2−N/2. (3.6)

Thus every KR is compact, and the suprema of the continuous finite-sample processes below are finite and measurable.

For a metric space (T,d), let γ2⁢(T,d) denote Talagrand’s chaining functional

inf(Ak)supt∈T∑k≥02k/2⁢diamd⁡(Ak⁢(t)), (3.7)

where (Ak)k≥0 ranges over nested partitions of T, Ak⁢(t) is the cell containing t, |A0|=1, and |Ak|≤22k [19].

We state explicitly the external covering fact used at the transition scales. For w∈R>0N, write W=diag⁡(w) and vi⁢(w)2:=aiT⁢(AT⁢W⁢A)−1⁢ai.

Lemma 3.2 (Imported multiscale covering primitive).

Fix α≥1, and let I⊂Z be a finite contiguous interval with |I|≥4⁢log⁡(2⁢N). Suppose that {w(j):j∈I} is an α-approximate Huber weight scheme, meaning that for every i and j∈I,

2jα≤H⁡(vi⁢(w(j)))wi(j)⁢vi⁢(w(j))2≤α⁢2j, (3.8)

and wi(j+1)≤α⁢wi(j) whenever j,j+1∈I. Put

λi(j):=wi(j)⁢vi⁢(w(j))2,τiI:=maxj∈I⁡λi(j),kI:=max⁡I+1. (3.9)

Define the associated deterministic metric by

dτI⁢(h,k):=maxi≤N⁡H⁡(aiT⁢(h−k))τiI. (3.10)

Then ∑iλi(j)=r, and for a constant Cα depending only on α,

γ2⁢(K2kI,dτI)≤Cα⁢2kI/2⁢log3/2⁡(2⁢N). (3.11)

There is a universal numerical α0 for which such a scheme has a deterministic finite construction from (A,I) in the exact-real model.

This is the specialization of the weight-scheme covering argument of Jambulapati et al. [12, Definition 1.8 and proof of Theorem 3.14] to fi=H; the cited bibliography entry identifies the arXiv full version in which those items appear. The assumptions hold because H is even, ψH=H is even and one-auto-Lipschitz by (3.2), and ψH is 1-auto-Lipschitz and has lower-1/2, upper-1 scaling while H has lower-1, upper-2 scaling: for λ≥1,

λ⁢H⁢(t)≤H⁡(λ⁢t)≤λ2⁢H⁢(t). (3.12)

The exact construction and its stopping criterion are recorded in Section A.1; no bit-complexity or running-time conclusion is used here.

The smallest and largest Huber radii reduce to ℓ22 and ℓ1 geometry. To avoid conflating Lewis scores with the auxiliary diagonal weights, for p∈{1,2} let ℓ(p)∈R>0N satisfy

ℓi(p)=(aiT⁢(AT⁢Lp1−2/p⁢A)−1⁢ai)p/2,Lp:=diag⁡(ℓ(p)). (3.13)

Set wi(p):=(ℓi(p))1−2/p and (vi(p))2:=aiT⁢(AT⁢diag⁡(w(p))⁢A)−1⁢ai. Then the weighted leverage is

λi(p):=wi(p)⁢(vi(p))2=(vi(p))p=ℓi(p). (3.14)

Thus w(2)≡1 and λ(2) is ordinary leverage, whereas λ(1) is the ℓ1 Lewis score.

Lemma 3.3 (Endpoint coverings).

For p∈{1,2}, the scores in (3.14) have total mass r. With

dp⁢(h,k):=maxi≤N⁡|aiT⁢(h−k)|p/2λi(p), (3.15)

they satisfy, for every S>0,

γ2⁢({h:‖A⁢h‖pp≤S},dp)≤C⁢S⁢log3/2⁡(2⁢N). (3.16)

The proof verifies an exact scale scheme using wi(j)=2−2j/pwi(p); see Section A.2. We now combine these endpoint scores with a finite transition-scale scheme.

Lemma 3.4 (All-radius label-oblivious Huber scores).

There are scores τi=τi⁢(A)>0, computable from A alone, such that

T:=∑i=1Nτi≤C⁢r⁢log⁡(2⁢N) (3.17)

and, for

dτ⁢(h,k):=maxi≤N⁡H⁡(aiT⁢(h−k))τi, (3.18)

one has, simultaneously for every R>0,

γ2⁢(KR,dτ)≤C⁢R⁢log3/2⁡(2⁢N). (3.19)
Proof.

Let

L:=⌈4⁢log⁡(2⁢N)⌉,J:=[−2−L, 2+⌈log2⁡(2⁢N)⌉]∩Z,

and construct the transition-scale schemes of Lemma 3.2 on J, denoting their weighted leverages by λi,tr(j). For p=1,2, let c≥1 be a fixed universal constant and let λ~i(p) be a deterministic finite constant-factor approximation to the exact endpoint score in Lemma 3.3, with λ~i(p)∈[λi(p)/c,c⁢λi(p)], and set λ¯i(p):=c⁢λ~i(p). Such approximations follow from the standard Lewis-weight construction [5]; for p=2, ordinary leverage can instead be used exactly. Define

τitr:=maxj∈J⁡λi,tr(j),τi:=τitr+λ¯i(1)+λ¯i(2).

Each inflated endpoint vector dominates its exact counterpart and has mass at most c2⁢r. Since every transition leverage vector has mass r,

∑iτi≤|J|⁢r+2⁢c2⁢r=O⁡(r⁢log⁡(2⁢N)).

Suppose first that 1/2≤R≤N/2, let kR:=⌈log2⁡R⌉, and put IR:={kR−L,…,kR−1}. Then IR⊆J, KR⊆K2kR, and dτ≤dτIR. Therefore Lemma 3.2 and 2kR<2⁢R give

γ2⁢(KR,dτ)≤γ2⁢(K2kR,dτIR)≤C⁢R⁢log3/2⁡(2⁢N).

If R≤1/2, every coordinate on KR lies in the quadratic region and

KR={h:‖Ah‖22≤2R},dτ≤2−1/2d2.

If R≥N/2, the bound H⁡(t)≥|t|−1/2 gives

KR⊆{h:‖A⁢h‖1≤R+N/2}⊆{h:‖A⁢h‖1≤2⁢R},dτ≤d1,

where the metric inequality uses H⁡(t)≤|t|. Applying Lemma 3.3 in both cases completes the proof. ∎

Set πimain:=τi/T, draw ν1,…,νm independently from πmain, and define

Φ^⁢(h):=1m⁢∑j=1mH⁡(aνjT⁢h)πνjmain. (3.20)

Sampling from the all-radius scores yields the unshifted estimate that will control the random envelope in the affine transfer.

Lemma 3.5 (Unshifted expectation bound).

There is an absolute constant C such that, whenever

α:=C⁢T⁢log3⁢(2⁢N)m≤1, (3.21)

then, for every R>0,

E⁢suph∈KR|Φ^⁢(h)−Φ⁡(h)|≤2⁢α⁢R. (3.22)

In particular,

E⁢suph∈KRΦ^⁢(h)≤3⁢R. (3.23)
Proof.

Let the left side of (3.22) be X. Ghost-sample symmetrization gives

X≤2⁢Eν,σ⁢suph∈KR|1m⁢∑j=1mσj⁢H⁡(aνjT⁢h)πνjmain|,

where the σj’s are independent Rademacher signs. Conditional on the indices, the increment metric is

dν⁢(h,k)2:=∑j=1m[H⁡(aνjT⁢h)−H⁡(aνjT⁢k)]2m2⁢(πνjmain)2.

Put Lν:=supu∈KRΦ^⁢(u). By (3.3), πimain=τi/T, and (3.18),

dν⁢(h,k)2≤2⁢Tm⁢dτ⁢(h,k)2⁢(Φ^⁢(h)+Φ^⁢(k))≤4⁢T⁢Lνm⁢dτ⁢(h,k)2.

The signed process is anchored at zero and has subgaussian increments in dν. The standard generic-chaining bound, followed by Lemma 3.4, therefore yields

Eσ⁢suph∈KR|Zh|≤C⁢γ2⁢(KR,dν)≤C⁢T⁢log3⁢(2⁢N)m⁢R⁢Lν,

where Zh=m−1⁢∑jσj⁢H⁢(aνjT⁢h)/πνjmain. Taking expectation over the indices and using Jensen gives

X≤α⁢R⁢E⁢Lν≤α⁢R⁢R+X,

because Lν≤R+supKR|Φ^−Φ|. For R>0, writing y=X/R gives y2≤α2⁢(1+y); its positive root is at most 2⁢α when α≤1. The case R=0 is immediate. Consequently X≤2⁢α⁢R and E⁢Lν≤R+X≤3⁢R. ∎

3.2 Centered affine preservation

The preceding scores control an unshifted empirical process. We now show that the same distribution preserves centered increments around any fixed residual, which is precisely what the second batch will require.

Fix an arbitrary residual vector z∈RN, and define

Fz⁢(h):=∑i=1NH⁡(zi+aiT⁢h),R:=Fz⁢(0)=∑i=1NH⁡(zi). (3.24)

Using a fresh sample from πmain, define

F^z⁢(h):=1m⁢∑j=1mH⁡(zνj+aνjT⁢h)πνjmain. (3.25)

Only sampled residual coordinates are needed. The same indices define F^z and Φ^; this coupling is used below.

Lemma 3.6 (Centered affine transfer).

Fix B≥1, 0<η,δ<1/2, and z with R=Fz⁢(0)>0. There is a constant C¯B=O⁡(B2), depending only on B, such that if

m≥C¯B⁢T⁢log3⁢(2⁢N)η2⁢δ2, (3.26)

then, with probability at least 1−δ over the fresh main sample,

suph∈KB⁢R|[F^z⁢(h)−F^z⁢(0)]−[Fz⁢(h)−Fz⁢(0)]|≤η⁢R. (3.27)

The distribution depends on A, but not on z.

Proof.

Set φi⁢(h):=H⁡(zi+aiT⁢h)−H⁡(zi). Since φi⁢(0)=0, signed symmetrization gives

E⁢suph∈KB⁢R|1m⁢∑j=1mφνj⁢(h)πνjmain−∑i=1Nφi⁢(h)|
≤2⁢Eν,σ⁢suph∈KB⁢R|1m⁢∑j=1mσj⁢φνj⁢(h)πνjmain|.

Conditional on the sampled indices, let dν,z be the increment metric of this signed process and put Lν,z:=supu∈KB⁢RF^z⁢(u). Exactly as in the unshifted calculation,

dν,z⁢(h,k)2≤2⁢Tm⁢dτ⁢(h,k)2⁢(F^z⁢(h)+F^z⁢(k))≤4⁢T⁢Lν,zm⁢dτ⁢(h,k)2.

The shared-sample weighted triangle inequality gives, pointwise,

F^z⁢(u)≤2⁢F^z⁢(0)+2⁢Φ^⁢(u).

After increasing the sample constant so that Lemma 3.5 applies on KB⁢R, unbiasedness and (3.23) yield

E⁢Lν,z≤(2+6⁢B)⁢R.

Generic chaining and Lemma 3.4, followed by Jensen, now bound the expected centered error by

DB⁢R⁢T⁢log3⁢(2⁢N)m,DB:=C⁢B⁡(2+6⁢B)=O⁡(B).

Choose C¯B at least DB2 times a sufficiently large universal constant, also large enough for the unshifted condition α≤1. Under (3.26), the last display is at most δ⁢η⁢R. Markov’s inequality gives (3.27). ∎

Remark 3.7 (Why centering matters).

The conclusion is additive, localized, and centered. It does not claim that F^z multiplicatively preserves the full affine objective. An unseen residual may make the common baseline arbitrarily large, but it cannot destroy preservation of the increments needed for optimization.

3.3 Pilot, algorithm, and localization

We next obtain the center needed by the transfer lemma and then state the full nonadaptive procedure. The pilot adapts the constant-factor mechanism of Musco et al. [14, Lemma 5.1], instantiated with the all-radius A-only embedding above and with the finite-scale bookkeeping made explicit.

The finite-range pilot estimate must be extended without using the false implication H⁡(t)≤1⇒|t|≤1. The following Huber-specific lemma uses the sharp quadratic threshold 1/2.

4 Matching lower bound against adaptive queries

This section proves Theorem 2.2, including algorithms with private randomness and stopping-time query counts. We first isolate the scalar Huber gap, then combine binary rate–distortion with a finite adaptive-transcript bound in two block-length regimes.

4.1 Scalar Huber gap

Lemma 4.1 (Exact scalar majority gap).

Let N≥1 and 0<γ≤1/2, and define

Fγ⁢(t):=N⁡(1/2−γ)⁢H⁢(4⁢t)+N⁡(1/2+γ)⁢H⁢(4⁢(t−1)). (4.1)

Put

qγ:=1/2−γ1/2+γ,tγ∗:=1−qγ4. (4.2)

Then tγ∗ is a global minimizer, and every t≤1/2 satisfies

Fγ⁢(t)−Fγ⁢(tγ∗)≥2⁢γ⁢N⁢1+4⁢γ1+2⁢γ≥2⁢γ⁢N. (4.3)

For a negative empirical majority, the symmetric statement holds with t↦1−t.

Proof.

At tγ∗, the zero-label residual is 4⁢tγ∗=4−qγ>1, while the one-label residual is 4⁢(tγ∗−1)=−qγ∈[−1,0]. Hence

Fγ′⁢(tγ∗)=4⁢N⁢[(1/2−γ)−(1/2+γ)⁢qγ]=0,

and convexity makes tγ∗ globally optimal. Also Fγ′⁢(1/2)=−8⁢γ⁢N<0, so the minimum on the wrong half-line (−∞,1/2] is attained at 1/2. Direct substitution gives

Fγ⁢(1/2) =3⁢N/2,
Fγ⁢(tγ∗) =N⁡[72⁢(1/2−γ)−(1/2−γ)22⁢(1/2+γ)],
Fγ⁢(1/2)−Fγ⁢(tγ∗) =2⁢γ⁢N⁢1+4⁢γ1+2⁢γ.

This proves (4.3); reflection about 1/2 gives the negative-majority case. ∎

4.2 Information lemmas for adaptive queries

For discrete random variables, H, I, and DKL denote Shannon entropy, mutual information, and KL divergence in nats. Define

h2nat(u):=−ulogu−(1−u)log(1−u),0log0:=0,dH(s,s^):=∑j=1r1{sj≠s^j}. (4.4)
Lemma 4.2 (Binary rate–distortion).

Let S be uniform on {−1,+1}r, and let S^ be any {−1,+1}r-valued estimate jointly distributed with S. If E⁢dH⁢(S,S^)<0.04⁢r, then

I⁡(S,S^)≥cRD⁢r,cRD:=log⁡2−h2nat⁢(0.04)>0.525. (4.5)
Proof.

For ej:=P[S^j≠Sj], the entropy chain rule and the binary Fano bound give

H⁡(S∣S^)≤∑j=1rh2nat⁢(ej)≤r⁢h2nat⁢(r−1⁢∑jej).

Since H⁡(S)=r⁢log⁡2, the claim follows by concavity of h2nat [6]. ∎

The next lemma accounts for the seed, adaptive index choices, caching, and stopping without an infinite transcript. Once repetitions are served from the cache, at most n fresh coordinates can be queried.

Lemma 4.3 (Finite adaptive-transcript bound).

Let S be any random variable, let B=(B1,…,Bn) be a random response vector, and let R be a private seed independent of (S,B). Suppose an adaptive algorithm returns almost surely after Q fresh coordinate queries to B. For t=1,…,n, let Zt=† if Q<t, and otherwise let Zt=(Jt,BJt) record the t-th fresh index and response. Put Ft−1:=σ⁡(R,Z1,…,Zt−1). The active indicator and Jt on the active event are Ft−1-measurable. For an active history h, let Ih denote mutual information under the regular conditional law given h. If Ih⁢(S,BJt⁢(h))≤κ for almost every active history, then

I(S;R,Z1:n)≤κEQ. (4.6)
Proof.

The seed is independent of S, and a stopped slot is deterministic. For each slot, the pointwise hypothesis and measurability of its active indicator and index give

I⁡(S;Zt∣Ft−1)≤κ⁢P⁢(Q≥t).

The chain rule therefore gives

I(S;R,Z1:n)=∑t=1nI(S;Zt∣Ft−1)≤κ∑t=1nP(Q≥t)=κEQ.

∎

4.3 Proof of the lower bound

Proof of Theorem 2.2.

We prove a distributional lower bound and then average to a fixed response. Fix n,d,r,ε, put N:=⌊n/r⌋, and use only the first r columns of the design. If the expected query count under either hard distribution below is infinite, the conclusion is immediate; otherwise the algorithm returns almost surely and Lemma 4.3 applies. For either construction, decode an output by setting S^j=+1 when x^j>1/2 and S^j=−1 otherwise. The decoder is measurable with respect to (R,Z1:n).

Short blocks: N<160.

Let the first r rows of A be the standard basis rows e1T,…,erT, and set the remaining rows to zero. Draw S uniformly from {−1,+1}r, set bj=Sj for j≤r, and set the padded responses to zero. The optimum is zero, so on the success event a multiplicative approximation must recover every Sj. The binary decoder therefore satisfies E⁢dH⁢(S,S^)≤0.01⁢r. Each fresh informative response carries at most log⁡2 nats, while padded rows are deterministic. Data processing and Lemmas 4.2 and 4.3 give

E⁢Q≥(cRD/log⁡2)⁢r.

Because n<160⁢r and r/ε2≥n, this is Ω⁡(min⁡{n,r/ε2}).

Long blocks: N≥160.

Set

β:=max⁡{200⁢ε,10N}. (4.7)

Then N⁢β2≥10 and β≤1/4. Conditional on independent uniform signs S1,…,Sr, draw independent bits

Yj⁢k∼Ber⁡(1/2+β⁢Sj),j∈[r],k∈[N],

and give row (j,k) the design vector 4⁢ejT and response 4⁢Yj⁢k. Pad the fewer than r remaining rows with zeros. A block is good when Sj⁢(Y¯j−1/2)≥β/2, where Y¯j:=N−1⁢∑k=1NYj⁢k; Hoeffding’s inequality gives P⁡(block j is not good∣Sj)≤e−5.

On a good block, Lemma 4.1 charges at least β⁢N excess loss for a wrong sign. The vector whose first r coordinates equal 1/2 has loss 3⁢N⁢r/2. For the realized response put F∗:=minx⁡Fb⁢(x); on the success event

Fb⁢(x^)−F∗≤(2⁢ε+ε2)⁢F∗≤92⁢ε⁢N⁢r.

Since β≥200⁢ε, at most 9⁢r/400 good blocks are decoded incorrectly. Charging all nongood blocks and all blocks on the failure event yields

E⁢dH⁢(S,S^)≤(e−5+9/400+0.01)⁢r<0.04⁢r. (4.8)

It remains to bound the information in one adaptive response. Let Ps:=Ber⁡(1/2+β⁢s). For Ft−1-almost every active history h, a padded-row response is deterministic and carries zero information. Otherwise the fresh index is the fixed pair Jt⁢(h)=(j,k). Under the corresponding regular conditional law, freshness gives the Markov relation S−j→Sj→Yj⁢k, and the observed response 4⁢Yj⁢k is a bijective function of Yj⁢k. Put θs⁢(h):=P⁡(Sj=s∣h). Convexity of KL in its second argument gives, under this conditional law,

Ih⁢(S,Yj⁢k) =Ih⁢(Sj,Yj⁢k)
≤∑s,s′∈{−1,+1}θs(h)θs′(h)DKL(Ps∥Ps′)≤16β2, (4.9)

E⁢Q≥cRD16⁢β2⁢r. (4.10)

rβ2=min⁡{r40000⁢ε2,r⁢N10}≥c⁢min⁡{rε2,n}. (4.11)

5 Related work and limitations

Active regression.

For least squares, leverage sampling gives an O⁡(r⁢log⁡r) constant-accuracy baseline and spectral sparsification reaches linear dependence on the effective dimension [4]. Lewis-weight sampling gives nearly tight guarantees for least absolute deviations [2, 15]. After column-space reduction, the nonadaptive Huber specialization of Musco et al. [14] uses O~⁢(r4−2⁢2⁢poly⁡(1/ε)) labels; our gain is the linear rank exponent and the matching joint dependence on n,r,ε. Stratified, semi-supervised, online, and single-index regression use different models [17, 16, 3, 8, 13, 10]; statistical Huber regression instead studies population risk [9, 18].

Sparsification and the contribution boundary.

Lewis weights and sensitivity sampling underlie subspace and objective summaries [5, 7]. Jambulapati et al. [11] developed common-basepoint signed chaining, and Jambulapati et al. [12] gave multiscale generalized-linear-model sparsifiers, including the unshifted Huber case. Full-data sparsification does not directly give active sampling because the affine augmentation contains hidden labels. The step specific to this paper is Lemma 3.6: after an independent pilot, design-only scores preserve the localized affine increments needed by the optimizer. We do not claim the pilot, localization, common-basepoint chaining, or unshifted sparsification separately. A fixed label-independent weighted proper-subset objective also cannot preserve every affine baseline, and optimizing either endpoint surrogate can be polynomially worse. The similarly titled work of Cavazza and Murino [1] concerns semi-supervised multi-view learning.

Proposition 5.1 (Simple surrogate minimizers can be polynomially bad).

For all sufficiently large N, there are one-dimensional instances on which the exact least-squares minimizer has Huber-norm approximation ratio Θ⁡(N1/4), and instances on which the exact ℓ1-regression minimizer has ratio Θ⁡(N).

The explicit constructions are given in Appendix B.

Limitations.

The result counts labels under exact real arithmetic and exact convex optimization; it gives neither a near-linear-time implementation, a strong affine coreset, nor optimal confidence dependence. Markov’s inequality causes the δ−2 factor, while the scale union, chaining, and pilot cause the logarithms. Known Huber sensitivity examples have Ω⁡(r⁢log⁡log⁢(n/r)) total sensitivity in some ranges [14], although this is not an active-query lower bound for those logarithms. Reducing the logarithmic and confidence factors, obtaining a fast implementation, and extending the centered transfer to other robust losses remain open.

A.3 Attainment and deterministic selections

For completeness, consider a sampled objective

G⁡(x)=∑i∈Sωi⁢H⁢(aiT⁢x−βi),ωi>0.
