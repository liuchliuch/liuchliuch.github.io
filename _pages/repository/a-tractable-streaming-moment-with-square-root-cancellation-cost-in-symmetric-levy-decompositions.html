A tractable streaming moment with square-root cancellation cost
in symmetric Lévy decompositions

Abstract

We construct a fixed computable nonnegative even function f on the integers whose moment Ff⁢(x)=∑if⁡(xi) admits a small-space turnstile sketch, but whose approximation by differences of real symmetric Lévy–Khintchine exponents requires large cancellation. The function satisfies f⁡(0)=0 and f⁡(k)∈{1,3} for k≠0. Its sketch uses space polynomial in inverse relative accuracy and in the logarithms of dimension, final frequency bound, and inverse failure probability. On the integers [−n,n], the cancellation cost of a split h=g+−g− is the least ρ such that g++g−≤ρ⁢f. Among splits satisfying |h−f|≤η⁢f, the minimum cost is Θη⁢(n) for every fixed 0<η<1/6; an exact finite Fourier split supplies the matching upper bound. This refutes the real-even integer specialization of the controlled-decomposition conjecture of Pettie and Wang (arXiv 2026). The witness uses Rudin–Shapiro signs: their order-n pairing with the target’s second differences contrasts with the O⁡(n) uniform norm of their cosine polynomial. A positive-measure representation of second differences turns this contrast into a lower bound for the entire symmetric Lévy cone, including arbitrary jump measures and Gaussian parts.

1 Introduction

A turnstile stream maintains an integer vector x under coordinate increments and decrements. For a fixed function f, moment estimation asks for a relative approximation to Ff⁢(x)=∑if⁡(xi) using small space. When f⁡(0)=0 and 1≤f⁡(k)≤3 for k≠0, a sample of the nonzero coordinates is enough, provided its weights can be evaluated in small space: every sampled weight is bounded, and the total weight is comparable to the support size. This argument places no restriction on how the values f⁡(1),f⁡(2),… are arranged. We show that their arrangement can nevertheless obstruct a proposed analytic characterization of tractability.

The Fourier–Hahn–Lévy construction of Pettie and Wang represents moments by differences of Lévy–Khintchine exponents [1, 2]. For an exact split f=g+−g−, estimates of the two component moments with relative error α have combined absolute error at most α⁢∑i(g+⁢(xi)+g−⁢(xi)). Thus a bound g++g−≤ρ⁢f permits relative error ε by estimating the components to accuracy ε/ρ. The size of ρ, not merely the existence of a signed representation, determines whether this reduction preserves a small-space guarantee. A Fourier expansion always provides such a representation on a finite integer interval, but need not control the cancellation uniformly as the interval grows.

We exhibit one fixed computable function with nonzero values in {1,3} for which the smallest cancellation cost is Θ⁡(n) on [−n,n], even after allowing a small fixed relative approximation error. The function is a constant plus the Rudin–Shapiro sequence. Its streaming algorithm only needs to evaluate a sampled sign; its analytic representation must reproduce the pattern of all the signs. The lower bound allows both exponents to depend on n and the approximation error, so selecting a different decomposition at each scale does not remove the gap.

Proving that one Fourier split has large mass would not establish this claim: another set of frequencies, or a continuous jump measure, might give a cheaper split. We instead test every symmetric Lévy exponent through its centered second differences. These are cosine coefficients of a positive measure whose total mass is the exponent’s value at 1. A cosine polynomial therefore bounds the corresponding pairing with D⁡(g+−g−) by its uniform norm times g+⁢(1)+g−⁢(1). Rudin–Shapiro signs supply both sides of the separation: linearly many sign changes give a pairing of order n, whereas the polynomial has norm O⁡(n). The pairing loses only O⁡(η⁢n) under relative error η, which is why approximation does not avoid the obstruction. Parseval’s identity gives an exact Fourier split of matching cost.

The pointwise controlled-decomposition question is Conjecture 1 of the expanded Pettie–Wang manuscript [2, Section 8.1]; we address its real-even integer specialization below. Wang’s harmonic sketch also uses a domination condition for signed combinations [3, Theorem 1 and Remark 3]. Our example shows why such an analytic condition cannot be inferred from streaming tractability alone. General turnstile characterizations, such as [4], impose no Lévy representation, and the bounded weights here require only the support-sampling methods of [5, 6].

1.1 Model and separation theorem

Let Lsym be the cone of real symmetric Lévy–Khintchine exponents

g⁡(x)=A⁢x2+∫0∞(1−cos⁡(x⁢s))⁢ν⁢(ds),A≥0,∫0∞min⁡{s2,1}⁢ν⁢(ds)<∞, (1.1)

where ν is a positive Borel measure on (0,∞). Every g∈Lsym is even and nonnegative, and g⁡(0)=0. For an even function f:Z→[0,∞) with f⁡(0)=0, an integer n≥1, and 0≤η<1, define

ρηf⁢(n):=inf{ρ≥0:there exist g+,g−∈Lsym such that, for every integer |k|≤n,|g+⁢(k)−g−⁢(k)−f⁡(k)|≤η⁢f⁢(k),g+⁢(k)+g−⁢(k)≤ρ⁢f⁢(k)}. (1.2)

The infimum of the empty set is +∞. We call ρ the cancellation cost; g++g− is the corresponding cancellation envelope. The case η=0 requires exact agreement on the specified integers. No condition is imposed outside this range. Formulating the constraints as inequalities also covers targets that vanish at additional integers.

Write Teven+ for the fixed nonnegative even functions f with f⁡(0)=0 for which the following holds. For every dimension d≥1, integer frequency bound U≥1, accuracy 0<ε≤1, and failure probability 0<δ<1/3, the moment

Ff⁢(x)=∑i=1df⁡(xi),x∈Zd,‖x‖∞≤U,

admits a one-pass turnstile sketch using

poly⁡(ε−1,log⁡(2⁢d),log⁡(2⁢U),log⁡(1/δ))bits (1.3)

and returning a (1±ε)-estimate with probability at least 1−δ. The vector is initially zero, updates have the form xi←xi+Δ with Δ∈Z, and the stream is fixed independently of the sketch’s randomness. The promise on U concerns the final vector; no bound on intermediate frequencies is needed for our construction. Space includes the random seed and decoding workspace, but not the read-only binary encoding of an incoming update. Unless a base is indicated, log denotes the natural logarithm.

The real-even integer formulation of the controlled-decomposition question asks whether

f∈Teven+⟹ρηf(n)≤qf(η−1,log(2n))(n≥1, 0<η<1) (1.4)

for some polynomial qf depending only on f. This is the real-symmetric specialization of Conjecture 1 in the expanded 2026 version of Pettie–Wang [2]. Their formulation uses a common size parameter for dimension and frequency range; keeping these parameters separate in (1.3) does not change the implication under consideration when both are polynomially bounded in that parameter.

Our counterexample uses the Rudin–Shapiro sequence

rk =(−1)a⁡(k)(k≥0), (1.5)
a⁡(k) =#⁢{overlapping 11 patterns in the binary expansion of k}.

Define

f(0)=0,f(k)=2+r|k|∈{1,3}(k≠0). (1.6)

This is a single computable function, not a family of tables indexed by the cutoff.

Theorem 1.1.

The function f in (1.6) belongs to Teven+. For every integer n≥15 and every 0<η<1/6,

(1−6⁢η)⁢n+18⁢2<ρηf⁢(n)≤ρ0f⁢(n)≤6⁢2⁢n+1. (1.7)

The upper bound ρ0f⁢(n)≤6⁢2⁢n+1 holds for every n≥1. Consequently, for each fixed 0<η<1/6, ρηf⁢(n)=Θη⁢(n), and (1.4) is false.

For example, at η=1/100 the theorem gives ρ1/100f⁢(n)>n+1/24 for every n≥15. With dimension and final frequency bound at most n, the streaming space at fixed accuracy is polynomial in log⁡n, while the cancellation cost grows as n. The theorem concerns precisely the envelope in (1.2); it does not give a lower bound for estimators that use correlations between components or a different complex or asymmetric representation.

2 Second differences and the Rudin–Shapiro certificate

The jump measure in (1.1) can have infinite mass near the origin, so its total variation is not itself a suitable measure of cancellation. Second differencing inserts the integrable factor 1−cos⁡s. The resulting finite measure retains all the integer values needed for the lower bound, and its mass is already controlled by the cancellation constraint at the single integer 1.

For a function u on Z, write

D⁢u⁢(k)=u⁡(k+1)−2⁢u⁢(k)+u⁡(k−1)2. (2.1)

The factor of 1/2 makes D⁢g⁢(0)=g⁡(1) for an even function vanishing at zero. The next identity extends this normalization to all cosine coefficients.

Lemma 2.1 (Positive second-difference measure).

For every g∈Lsym, there is a finite positive measure λg on T=R/(2⁢π⁢Z) such that

D⁢g⁢(k)=∫Tcos⁡(k⁢t)⁢λg⁢(dt)(k∈Z),λg⁢(T)=g⁡(1). (2.2)
Proof.

Take the pushforward of (1−cos⁡s)⁢ν⁢(d⁢s) under reduction modulo 2⁢π, and add A⁢δ0, where A and ν are from (1.1). This defines a positive finite measure because 1−cos⁡s≤2⁢min⁡{s2,1}, and its mass is

λg⁢(T)=A+∫0∞(1−cos⁡s)⁢ν⁢(ds)=g⁡(1).

The identities

D⁡(A⁢k2)=A,D⁡(1−cos⁡(k⁢s))=(1−cos⁡s)⁢cos⁡(k⁢s)

now give (2.2). Passing the finite difference through the integral is legitimate: each original integrand is integrable at the relevant integer, and the resulting integrand is bounded in absolute value by the integrable function 1−cos⁡s. The measure ν itself need not be finite. ∎

Corollary 2.2 (Dual inequality).

Let h=g+−g− and s=g++g−, with g+,g−∈Lsym. For every finitely supported real sequence (ak)k∈Z,

|∑kak⁢D⁢h⁢(k)|≤s⁡(1)⁢‖∑kak⁢cos⁡(k⁢t)‖L∞⁢(T). (2.3)
Proof.

The signed measure λg+−λg− has total variation at most g+⁢(1)+g−⁢(1)=s⁡(1). Integrating the cosine polynomial against this measure gives (2.3). ∎

Remark 2.3.

The inequality applies to Gaussian terms, finite or infinite jump measures, and both continuous and atomic measures. Its relation to the normalized Lévy cone can be made explicit. For integer k, put

Kt⁢(k)=1−cos⁡(k⁢t)1−cos⁡t(t≠0⁢ in ⁢T),K0⁢(k)=k2.

The value at 0 is the continuous limit, and the measure in Lemma 2.1 satisfies

g⁡(k)=∫TKt⁢(k)⁢λg⁢(dt).

Consequently, if g⁡(1)>0, then g⁡(k)/g⁡(1) is a probability mixture of the normalized generators Kt⁢(k). If g⁡(1)=0, then A=0 and ν is concentrated, up to a null set, on positive multiples of 2⁢π; hence g⁡(k)=0 at every integer. In particular, atoms invisible on the integers cannot affect the certificate, and no restriction to a discrete frequency grid is involved.

To use (2.3), the test coefficients must correlate with the second differences of f without adding coherently at any frequency. Alternating coefficients would fail: at t=π, every term (−1)k⁢cos⁡(k⁢t) equals one, and the dual norm is as large as the number of terms. The Rudin–Shapiro construction instead has uniform norm of square-root order. It also has linearly many sign changes, each of which contributes with the same sign to the second-difference pairing. We record both properties, including the endpoint terms needed when f⁡(0)=0.

Set P0⁢(z)=Q0⁢(z)=1 and define

Pm+1⁢(z) =Pm⁢(z)+z2m⁢Qm⁢(z), (2.4)
Qm+1⁢(z) =Pm⁢(z)−z2m⁢Qm⁢(z).

The coefficients of Pm are r0,…,r2m−1, as in the classical Rudin–Shapiro construction [7]. To verify the identification with (1.5), define

Rm⁢(z)=∑k=02m−1rk⁢zk,Sm⁢(z)=∑k=02m−1r2m+k⁢zk.

Prefixing binary words gives Rm+1=Rm+z2m⁢Sm and Sm+1=Rm−z2m⁢Sm. Indeed, the two halves of the word for Sm+1 are obtained by prefixing an m-bit word by 10 and 11, respectively; the first produces the signs in Rm, and the second reverses those in Sm. Since R0=S0=1, induction gives Rm=Pm and Sm=Qm.

Lemma 2.4 (Norm and transition counts).

Let N=2m. For every real t,

|Pm⁢(ei⁢t)|2+|Qm⁢(ei⁢t)|2=2⁢N,|Pm⁢(ei⁢t)|≤2⁢N. (2.5)

If Tm and Um count adjacent sign transitions in the coefficient words of Pm and Qm, then Tm+Um=N−1, and for m≥2,

Tm={N/2,m⁢ odd,N/2−1,m⁢ even. (2.6)
Proof.

For |z|=1, the parallelogram identity in (2.4) yields

|Pm+1⁢(z)|2+|Qm+1⁢(z)|2=2⁢(|Pm⁢(z)|2+|Qm⁢(z)|2).

The initial sum is 2, proving (2.5).

At the word level, (2.4) concatenates (Pm,Qm) and (Pm,−Qm). Exactly one of their two joins is a transition. Thus

Tm+1+Um+1=2⁢(Tm+Um)+1,

and the initial counts are zero, so Tm+Um=2m−1. Both words begin with 1. In the concatenation forming Pm, the left word ends with r2m−1−1=(−1)m−2, since 2m−1−1 has m−1 binary ones. For m≥2, this join is therefore a transition exactly when m is odd. Adding the internal transitions gives

Tm=Tm−1+Um−1+{1,m⁢ odd,0,m⁢ even,

which is (2.6). ∎

The certificate must avoid second differences involving the exceptional value f⁡(0)=0. For N=2m≥4, retain only the interior terms:

AN⁢(t)=∑k=2N−2rk⁢cos⁡(k⁢t),‖AN‖∞≤2⁢N+3. (2.7)

Indeed, it is the real part of Pm⁢(ei⁢t) after deleting the three terms at indices 0,1,N−1, each of modulus at most 1. On these indices, D⁢f=D⁢r. Moreover, −rk⁢D⁢r⁢(k) counts the sign changes on the two edges adjacent to k: a neighboring equal sign contributes zero and a different sign contributes one. Thus the transition count gives a linear pairing, with the following exact boundary correction.

Lemma 2.5 (Exact second-difference pairing).

For N=2m≥16,

∑k=2N−2rk⁢D⁢f⁢(k)={−N+1,m⁢ odd,−N+3,m⁢ even. (2.8)

In particular, the absolute value is at least N−3.

Proof.

Write bk=rk+1−2⁢rk+rk−1 and uj=rj⁢rj+1. A transition contributes −1 to uj, and a nontransition contributes 1, so

∑j=0N−2uj=N−1−2⁢Tm.

The endpoint signs are

r0=r1=r2=1,rN−2=(−1)m−2,rN−1=(−1)m−1.

Thus u0=1, uN−2=−1, and b1=0. Summing the two neighboring products in rk⁢bk gives

∑k=1N−2rk⁢bk =2⁢∑j=0N−2uj−u0−uN−2−2⁢(N−2)
=2−4⁢Tm. (2.9)

By (2.6), this equals −2⁢N+2 for odd m and −2⁢N+6 for even m. The k=1 term may be deleted because b1=0. For 2≤k≤N−2, all three arguments in the second difference are nonzero, and the constant 2 in (1.6) cancels. Hence D⁢f⁢(k)=bk/2, and division by 2 proves (2.8). Excluding k=1 is necessary for this last identity: the exceptional value f⁡(0)=0 prevents the constant part from cancelling there. ∎

3 Proof of the separation theorem

The certificate now gives the lower bound for any admissible pair. Because the target is bounded by 3, relative error η changes each second difference by at most 6⁢η. Its total effect is therefore smaller than the linear pairing whenever η<1/6. Only target values inside the prescribed interval are used; after applying the dual inequality, the envelope is needed only at 1.

Proposition 3.1 (Dyadic lower bound).

Let N=2m≥16, n=N−1, and 0≤η<1/6. If g+,g−∈Lsym satisfy

|g+(k)−g−(k)−f(k)|≤ηf(k),g+(k)+g−(k)≤ρf(k)(|k|≤n), (3.1)

then

ρ≥(1−6⁢η)⁢(N−3)3⁢(2⁢N+3). (3.2)

At η=1/100, this implies

ρ≥47⁢(N−3)150⁢(2⁢N+3)≥N6⁢(2⁢N+3)≥N12⁢2. (3.3)
Proof.

Put h=g+−g−, s=g++g−, and e=h−f. Since f⁡(j)≤3, the approximation condition gives |e⁡(j)|≤3⁢η for |j|≤n. Therefore, for 2≤k≤N−2,

|D⁢e⁢(k)|≤|e⁡(k+1)|+2⁢|e⁡(k)|+|e⁡(k−1)|2≤6⁢η.

All three indices lie in [1,N−1]. There are N−3 terms in the pairing, so Lemma 2.5 yields

|∑k=2N−2rk⁢D⁢h⁢(k)|≥(N−3)−6⁢η⁢(N−3)=(1−6⁢η)⁢(N−3). (3.4)

Applying Corollary 2.2 with ak=rk for 2≤k≤N−2 and zero elsewhere, and then using (2.7), gives

s⁡(1)≥(1−6⁢η)⁢(N−3)2⁢N+3.

On the other hand, f⁡(1)=3, so the cancellation constraint at 1 gives s⁡(1)≤3⁢ρ. This proves (3.2). For η=1/100, the first simplification in (3.3) uses 22⁢N≥141, and the second uses 3≤2⁢N, both valid for N≥16. ∎

Corollary 3.2 (All cutoffs).

For every n≥15 and 0≤η<1/6,

ρηf⁢(n)>(1−6⁢η)⁢n+18⁢2. (3.5)
Proof.

Take N=2⌊log2⁡(n+1)⌋. Then N≥16, N−1≤n, and N>(n+1)/2. Restricting an admissible pair to [−N+1,N−1] preserves both constraints. Thus Proposition 3.1 implies

ρηf⁢(n)≥(1−6⁢η)⁢(N−3)3⁢(2⁢N+3).

For N≥16,

N−3≥13⁢N16,2⁢N+3≤(2+34)⁢N<136⁢N.

Substitution gives

ρηf⁢(n)>(1−6⁢η)⁢N8>(1−6⁢η)⁢n+18⁢2,

as required. ∎

For the matching upper bound, it suffices to apply the periodic Fourier–Hahn construction [1, 2] on a group of size 2⁢n+1. The bounded values of f give a bounded mean-square Fourier norm. Cauchy–Schwarz then loses a factor 2⁢n+1 when passing to the absolute Fourier mass, exactly the scale forced by the certificate.

Proposition 3.3.

For every n≥1, there are g+,g−∈Lsym such that

g+(k)−g−(k)=f(k),g+(k)+g−(k)≤62⁢n+1f(k)(|k|≤n).
Proof.

Put p=2⁢n+1 and identify [−n,n]∩Z with Z/p⁢Z. Let F be the corresponding real even function, with normalized Fourier coefficients

cj=1p∑x=0p−1F(x)e−2πijx/p,0≤j<p.

Pairing x with −x shows that cj is real. Fourier inversion gives F⁡(k)=∑jcj⁢cos⁡(2⁢π⁢j⁢k/p), and F⁡(0)=0 implies ∑jcj=0. Hence

F⁡(k)=∑j=0p−1(−cj)⁢(1−cos⁡2⁢π⁢j⁢kp). (3.6)

Define aj+=max⁡{−cj,0}, aj−=max⁡{cj,0}, and

g±⁢(x)=∑j=1p−1aj±⁢(1−cos⁡2⁢π⁢j⁢xp),x∈R.

Both functions belong to Lsym, with finite positive atomic jump measures. The omitted j=0 term in (3.6) is identically zero, so g+−g−=f on the required integers.

Cauchy–Schwarz and Parseval give

∑j=0p−1|cj|≤p⁢(∑j=0p−1|cj|2)1/2=(∑x=0p−1F⁢(x)2)1/2≤3⁢p.

Since 0≤1−cos⁡θ≤2, the cancellation envelope is at most 2⁢∑j|cj|≤6⁢p. At a nonzero integer in the prescribed range, f⁡(k)≥1; at zero, the target and both exponents vanish. This proves the claimed pointwise bound. ∎

The exact split uses O⁡(n) atoms. It certifies the upper bound on cancellation, but is not the small-space algorithm for the moment. That algorithm recovers a sparse sample of the input coordinates and evaluates their weights directly. We finish by verifying that it works in the bit-space model of (1.3), including the promise on final rather than intermediate frequencies.

Proposition 3.4.

The function f in (1.6) is a fixed computable member of Teven+.

Proof.

Let S={i:xi≠0}, s=|S|, and F=Ff⁢(x). When s>0, s≤F≤3⁢s. If the coordinates are retained with probability p using pairwise independent indicators Bi, then

Z=1p⁢∑iBi⁢f⁢(xi),E⁢Z=F,Var⁡Z≤9⁢sp.

Thus a sample with expected support size of order ε−2 suffices for a relative estimate with constant success probability. Dyadic subsampling and sparse recovery implement this idea when s is unknown, following the support-sampling framework [5, 6].

For the precise final-frequency model used here, Lemma A.2 supplies a self-contained finite-field implementation, including the selection of a sampling level, recovery errors, and amplification to failure probability δ. It uses space (1.3) for any weight function taking values in {1,2,3} away from zero, provided those values can be evaluated in polylogarithmic space. The value r|k| is obtained by scanning the binary expansion of |k| and recording the parity of the number of adjacent pairs of ones. This requires O⁡(log⁡(2⁢U)) workspace when |k|≤U. The lemma therefore applies to f, including the zero vector, for which its output is exactly zero. ∎

Proof of Theorem 1.1.

Proposition 3.4 gives f∈Teven+. Corollaries 3.2 and 3.3, together with the inclusion of exact admissible pairs among approximate ones, give (1.7). For fixed 0<η<1/6, these bounds are of order n. If (1.4) held for this f, then at η=1/100 it would imply ρ1/100f⁢(n)≤qf⁢(100,log⁡(2⁢n)), contradicting (3.5) as n→∞. ∎

The separation depends on the sign pattern, not on large weights. For comparison, Appendix B constructs controlled splits of k2⁢log⁡(1+|k|), an example considered in [2], and more generally of fixed logarithmic powers multiplying |k|p, 0<p≤2. The first has cancellation cost O⁡(ε−1⁢log⁡n) at relative accuracy ε, despite its slightly superquadratic growth. Together these examples distinguish growth restrictions on a target from the oscillatory obstruction detected by the second-difference certificate.

Appendix A A finite-field support-sampling sketch

This appendix supplies the bit-space guarantee used in Proposition 3.4, without a bound on intermediate frequencies. At each sampling level, a linear summary either recovers the retained frequencies or reports that the sample is too dense. The usual dyadic subsampling strategy [5, 6] then chooses a level with enough retained coordinates for concentration. Finite-field arithmetic removes any dependence on intermediate frequency magnitudes; an independent checksum prevents a dense vector from being mistaken for a sparse one. The decoder below uses polynomial time in the dimension, the sparsity threshold, and the field bit length.

Lemma A.1 (Sparse recovery with a checksum).

Let d,U,K≥1 be integers, and let q>max⁡{d,2⁢U} be prime. There is a linear turnstile summary over Fq, using O⁡(K⁢log⁡q) bits, with the following guarantee for any fixed final vector v∈[−U,U]d∩Zd. If v has at most K nonzero coordinates, the decoder returns v exactly. Otherwise it returns dense, except with probability at most d/q. Decoding uses O⁡(K2⁢log⁡q) workspace and time polynomial in d,K,log⁡q.

Proof.

Store the 2⁢K power sums and one random checksum

Mt=∑i=1dvi⁢it(modq)(0≤t<2⁢K),Cz=∑i=1dvi⁢zi(modq), (A.1)

where z is uniform in Fq. These quantities are linear in the updates. The seed z, powers, and accumulators fit within O⁡(K⁢log⁡q) bits.

There is at most one vector y∈[−U,U]d∩Zd with at most K nonzero coordinates that matches all the Mt. Indeed, the difference of two candidates is supported on some ℓ≤min⁡{2⁢K,d} distinct indices. Its first ℓ power sums form a Vandermonde system over Fq, whose determinant is nonzero because the indices are distinct modulo q. Every coordinate of the difference is therefore zero modulo q. Its absolute value is at most 2⁢U<q, so the two candidates are equal as integer vectors.

We next describe how to find this candidate, if it exists, without using z or Cz. If all power sums are zero, take the zero candidate. Otherwise try each 1≤r≤min⁡{K,d}. If the Hankel matrix Hr=(Mt+j)0≤t,j<r is invertible, solve

∑j=0r−1cj⁢Mt+j=−Mt+r(0≤t<r),Pr⁢(X)=Xr+∑j=0r−1cj⁢Xj. (A.2)

Scan the indices 1,…,d for roots of Pr. If there are exactly r roots, solve the Vandermonde system on these indices using M0,…,Mr−1 to obtain their candidate values in Fq. Reject this trial if any value has no representative in [−U,U]. Otherwise take those representatives and check all 2⁢K power sums. Retain a trial only if every check passes. If no trial passes, there is no candidate found and the decoder returns dense.

To see that this procedure finds every valid sparse candidate, suppose y has support i1,…,ir and matches the power sums. Let Vt,a=iat for 0≤t<r. Then

Hr=V⁢diag⁡(yi1,…,yir)⁢VT

is invertible over Fq: the indices are distinct, and the nonzero values of y remain nonzero modulo q. The polynomial ∏a=1r(X−ia) satisfies (A.2), so it is precisely the polynomial found at this trial. Its roots and values therefore recover y. The zero candidate was handled separately, and uniqueness shows that two successful trials cannot produce different vectors.

Finally, return the candidate y only if it also matches Cz, and return dense otherwise. For a K-sparse v, the candidate is v and always passes. If v is not K-sparse, any candidate y differs from v and is determined independently of z. The polynomial

∑i=1d(vi−yi)⁢Zi∈Fq⁢[Z]

is nonzero and has degree at most d. It has at most d roots, so the probability of false acceptance is at most d/q.

The linear systems have dimension at most K, and a candidate uses at most K index-value pairs. Gaussian elimination, the scans over indices, and the checks therefore use O⁡(K2⁢log⁡q) workspace and polynomial time in the stated parameters. All stored summaries are reduced modulo q after each update. Since the final coordinates lie in [−U,U], arbitrary intermediate integer values do not affect the argument. ∎

We apply this recovery primitive at nested sampling levels. The level is selected from the sketch itself, so it is not enough to establish accuracy at one fixed level. The proof bounds the total probability of an inaccurate estimate over all levels that could be selected.

Lemma A.2 (Bounded-weight moments).

Let w:Z→{0,1,2,3} satisfy w⁡(0)=0 and 1≤w⁡(k)≤3 for k≠0. Suppose that w⁡(k) can be evaluated in space polynomial in log⁡(2+|k|). Then ∑iw⁡(xi) has a one-pass turnstile sketch with the space and success guarantees in (1.3), under the promise ‖x‖∞≤U on the final vector alone.

Proof.

We first construct one copy with success probability at least 3/4. Set

K=⌈2048⁢ε−2⌉,J=⌈log2⁡d⌉,ℓ=⌈log2⁡(d+1)⌉.

Choose a prime

B<q≤2⁢B,B=max⁡{2⁢U,64⁢d⁢(J+1)}. (A.3)

Such a prime exists by Bertrand’s postulate, and may be found by trial division in O⁡(log⁡B) space. In particular, log⁡q=O⁡(log⁡(2⁢d)+log⁡(2⁢U)).

Let ι⁡(i)∈F2ℓ be the length-ℓ binary representation of i. Choose a uniform matrix H∈F2J×ℓ and a uniform vector b∈F2J, independently, and put h⁡(i)=H⁢ι⁢(i)+b. For distinct indices the hash values are independent and uniform: the difference H⁡(ι⁡(i)−ι⁡(i′)) is uniform in F2J, while b independently makes either hash value uniform. At level 0≤j≤J, retain i when the first j bits of h⁡(i) are zero. The indicators Bi(j) are pairwise independent at each level, with retention probability pj=2−j. Level zero retains every coordinate.

At each level maintain the summary in Lemma A.1 for the sampled vector vi(j)=Bi(j)⁢xi, using independent checksums and the same prime q. All checksum seeds are independent of the hashing. At the end, decode the summaries and select the smallest level whose decoder does not return dense. If this level is j, let v^(j) be its recovered candidate and output

F^=2j⁢∑iw⁡(v^i(j)), (A.4)

summing over its recovered support. If every decoder returns dense, output zero. Each update can be processed by testing its index at every level and adding its contribution modulo q. Even an arbitrarily long binary encoding of Δ can be reduced modulo q as it is read, using O⁡(log⁡q) workspace.

Fix the stream, let s=|{i:xi≠0}|, and put F=∑iw⁡(xi). Conditioned on the hash function, the sampled vectors are fixed, and Lemma A.1 applies independently of that conditioning. A union bound gives probability at most

(J+1)⁢d/q<1/64 (A.5)

that any nonsparse sampled vector is falsely accepted. If s≤K, level zero always recovers the full vector, so the estimate is exact, including when s=0.

Suppose now that s>K, and define

j∗=⌈log2⁡(2⁢s/K)⌉.

Then 1≤j∗≤J and K/4<pj∗⁢s≤K/2. Write Sj=∑i:xi≠0Bi(j) for the sampled support size. Pairwise independence gives Var⁡Sj∗≤pj∗⁢s. Chebyshev’s inequality therefore yields

Pr⁡(Sj∗>K)≤pj∗⁢s(K−pj∗⁢s)2≤2K. (A.6)

For each 0≤j≤j∗, consider the estimator on the true sampled vector,

Zj=pj−1⁢∑iBi(j)⁢w⁢(xi).

It satisfies E⁢Zj=F, F≥s, and Var⁡Zj≤9⁢s/pj. Hence

Pr⁡(|Zj−F|>ε⁢F)≤9ε2⁢pj⁢s.

No independence between levels is required. Summing these failure probabilities gives

Pr(∃j≤j∗:|Zj−F|>εF)≤9ε2⁢s∑j=0j∗2j<18ε2⁢pj∗⁢s<72ε2⁢K. (A.7)

If none of the bad events bounded in (A.5)–(A.7) occurs, then level j∗ is recoverable, the selected level is at most j∗, and its recovered vector is correct. The output is therefore within (1±ε)⁢F. The total failure probability is less than

164+2K+72ε2⁢K<14.

Choose R to be the smallest odd integer at least 8⁢log⁡(1/δ). Run R independent copies and return their median. The probability that at least half the copies fail is at most e−R/8≤δ, by Hoeffding’s inequality applied to their failure indicators. The hash seed uses O⁡(J⁢ℓ) bits, and the summaries of one copy use O⁡((J+1)⁢K⁢log⁡q) bits. Sequential decoding uses an additional O⁡(K2⁢log⁡q) workspace, and evaluating w requires the polynomial in log⁡(2⁢U) from the hypothesis. Storing the R outputs for their median also fits within (1.3). This proves the stated space bound and success probability. ∎

Appendix B Controlled decompositions for logarithmic moments

The logarithm can be approximated by a small difference of powers: for small α, α−1⁢(1−e−α⁢y) approximates y. Multiplying this identity by x2 suggests a split of x2⁢log⁡(1+|x|) into a Gaussian exponent and a damped quadratic. The point requiring verification is that the damped term is itself a Lévy exponent. Bernstein-function calculus establishes this and also permits higher finite differences for fixed powers of the logarithm.

Recall that a Bernstein function ϕ is a nonnegative smooth function on (0,∞) with completely monotone derivative, meaning (−1)j⁢ϕ(j+1)⁢(t)≥0 for all integers j≥0. When ϕ⁡(0)=0, its representation has the form

ϕ⁡(t)=b⁢t+∫0∞(1−e−u⁢t)⁢μ⁢(du),b≥0,∫0∞min⁡{u,1}⁢μ⁢(du)<∞. (B.1)

Here μ is a positive measure on (0,∞). A complete Bernstein function is a Bernstein function whose measure μ has a completely monotone density. We use the equivalent analytic characterization: a nonnegative function on (0,∞) with a finite nonnegative limit at zero is a complete Bernstein function if it extends holomorphically to C∖(−∞,0] and has nonnegative imaginary part in the upper half-plane [8, Chapters 3 and 6].

For completeness, (B.1) explains why x↦ϕ⁡(x2) belongs to Lsym. For u>0, the Gaussian characteristic-function identity gives

1−e−u⁢x2=∫0∞(1−cos⁡(x⁢s))⁢e−s2/(4u)π⁢u⁢ds.

The density in this integral has mass 1 and second moment 2⁢u, so its integral against min⁡{s2,1} is at most min⁡{2⁢u,1}≤2⁢min⁡{u,1}. Integrating against μ and applying Tonelli’s theorem gives the integrability condition in (1.1), with Gaussian coefficient b. Thus

ϕ⁡(0)=0,ϕ⁢ Bernstein⟹ϕ⁡(x2)∈Lsym. (B.2)
Lemma B.1 (A complete Bernstein kernel).

Let 0<a,b≤1 and c≥0 satisfy b⁢c≤a. Then

ϕa,b,c⁢(t)=ta(1+tb)c,t≥0,

is a complete Bernstein function vanishing at zero.

Proof.

Use principal branches on C∖(−∞,0]. If z=r⁢ei⁢θ, with 0<θ<π, then

0<arg⁡(1+zb)<b⁢θ.

Indeed, 1+zb lies strictly inside the sector between the positive real axis and zb. It neither vanishes nor meets the negative real axis. Conjugation gives the corresponding statement in the lower half-plane, and the denominator is positive on the positive real axis. The quotient is therefore holomorphic on the slit plane.

The argument of the quotient is a⁢θ−c⁢arg⁡(1+zb). If c>0, it is strictly greater than (a−b⁢c)⁢θ≥0 and at most a⁢θ<π. If c=0, it equals a⁢θ∈(0,π). Thus the quotient maps the upper half-plane into itself. It is nonnegative on (0,∞) and tends to zero as t↓0. The preceding characterization proves the claim. ∎

Proposition B.2 (A single logarithm).

Let F⁡(k)=k2⁢log⁡(1+|k|), n≥2, and 0<ε<1/2. Set α=2⁢ε/log⁡(1+n). Then

G+⁢(x)=α−1⁢x2,G−⁢(x)=α−1⁢x2(1+|x|)α

belong to Lsym, and for every integer |k|≤n,

(1−ε)⁢F⁢(k) ≤G+⁢(k)−G−⁢(k)≤F⁡(k), (B.3)
G+⁢(k)+G−⁢(k) ≤log⁡(1+n)ε⁢log⁡2⁢F⁢(k). (B.4)
Proof.

The parameter satisfies 0<α<2. Applying Lemma B.1 with (a,b,c)=(1,1/2,α) shows that t⁢(1+t)−α is a Bernstein function vanishing at zero. Equation (B.2) therefore proves G−∈Lsym, and G+∈Lsym is a Gaussian exponent.

For k≠0, put y=α⁢log⁡(1+|k|). Then 0<y≤2⁢ε and

G+⁢(k)−G−⁢(k)F⁡(k)=1−e−yy.

The identity (1−e−y)/y=∫01e−t⁢y⁢dt, together with 1−t⁢y≤e−t⁢y≤1, gives

1−y/2≤1−e−yy≤1. (B.5)

This proves (B.3). Also,

G+⁢(k)+G−⁢(k)≤2⁢α−1⁢k2≤2α⁢log⁡2⁢F⁢(k),

which is (B.4). At zero, all the functions vanish. ∎

Proposition B.3 (Fixed logarithmic powers).

Fix 0<p,β≤2 and an integer m≥1. For every n≥2 and 0<ε<1/2, the function

Fp,β,m⁢(k)=|k|p⁢[log⁡(1+|k|β)]m

admits a relative (1−ε,1) approximation on [−n,n]∩Z by a difference of two elements of Lsym, with cancellation cost

Op,β,m⁢((ε−1⁢log⁡n)m).
Proof.

Set

a=p/2,b=β/2,α=min⁡{2⁢εm⁢log⁡(1+nβ),pm⁢β}.

For every 0≤j≤m, we have b⁢j⁢α≤a. By Lemma B.1 and (B.2),

Hj⁢(x)=|x|p⁢(1+|x|β)−j⁢α

belongs to Lsym. Define the positive and negative parts of the finite difference explicitly by

G+⁢(x)=α−m⁢∑0≤j≤mj⁢even(mj)⁢Hj⁢(x),G−⁢(x)=α−m⁢∑0≤j≤mj⁢odd(mj)⁢Hj⁢(x). (B.6)

Both functions belong to Lsym.

For k≠0, put y=α⁢log⁡(1+|k|β). The binomial theorem gives

G+⁢(k)−G−⁢(k)=|k|p⁢α−m⁢(1−e−y)m,

so the ratio to Fp,β,m⁢(k) equals ((1−e−y)/y)m. Since 0<y≤2⁢ε/m, (B.5) and Bernoulli’s inequality imply

1−ε≤1−m⁢y2≤(1−y/2)m≤(1−e−yy)m≤1.

For the cancellation envelope, Hj⁢(k)≤|k|p yields

G+⁢(k)+G−⁢(k)≤2m⁢α−m⁢|k|p.

At nonzero integers, log⁡(1+|k|β)≥log⁡2, and hence

G+⁢(k)+G−⁢(k)Fp,β,m⁢(k)≤(2log⁡2)m⁢α−m.

Finally,

α−1=max⁡{m⁢log⁡(1+nβ)2⁢ε,m⁢βp}=Op,β,m⁢(ε−1⁢log⁡n).

At zero, the target and both functions in (B.6) vanish. This proves both assertions. ∎
